# Federal and State Policy: the new Kwinana report against Port Hedland

Comparison of sections **3.1 Policy and Governance: Federal Policy** and
**3.2 Policy and Governance: State Policy** in the Kwinana Regional Readiness
Report draft supplied September 2026, against the Port Hedland evidence
currently live in `evidence-port-hedland.js`.

## Why most differences here are errors rather than local context

These twelve indicators assess the *same* Commonwealth government and the
*same* Western Australian government. Only the paragraphs that apply a policy
to a place should differ between the two reports. Everywhere the two documents
state a different fact about a federal program, a WA program, a figure or a
date, one of them is wrong — that is not a regional difference and cannot be
left to sit.

Two things make this round harder than a straight comparison:

1. **The new Kwinana draft is newer than anything in the repo.** Neither
   `evidence-kwinana.js` nor `evidence-port-hedland.js` mentions the Net Zero
   Plan, the Environment Protection Reform Act 2025, the Carbon Leakage
   Review, the Clean Energy Powerhouse Bill, the Guarantee of Origin scheme,
   the Investor Front Door, the Net Zero Fund or the repeal of the US
   Inflation Reduction Act credits. The whole federal picture has moved.
2. **Five of the six federal indicators on the Port Hedland pages are still
   Kwinana's text**, as `PORT-HEDLAND-DRAFT-ISSUES.md` records. So for those
   five, comparing the new Kwinana draft against the site is really comparing
   the new Kwinana draft against the *old* Kwinana draft. Every federal fact
   the new draft corrects is a fact currently wrong on Port Hedland's pages
   as well.

The September 2026 Port Hedland report already localises those five. This
review does not replace that decision — it adds the federal corrections the
Kwinana draft has since made, which the Port Hedland report may or may not
carry.

---

## 1. One score conflict — this needs your call first

**Federal — research and development.** `scores.js` has Port Hedland and
Kwinana both at **4**. The new Kwinana draft scores it **3**, and says so
explicitly:

> "assessed as a 3, downgraded from the 4 the evidence supported at the start
> of 2026 … three of the five programs on which the higher rating rested have
> since been cut, reduced or closed."

The reasons given are all federal and region-neutral: Solar Sunshot's
uncommitted funding clawed back, the Battery Breakthrough Initiative cut from
$523.2m to $142.32m and closed to new applications, and roughly $1.5 billion
in research funding cut or redirected in the 2026‑27 federal Budget with
Australia's Economic Accelerator abolished.

None of that is about Kwinana. If it holds, Port Hedland's federal R&D score
should move to 3 too. `PORT-HEDLAND-DRAFT-ISSUES.md` records that every score
in the September Port Hedland report matches `scores.js`, so the Port Hedland
report still says 4. **Two BZE reports in the same round would then give the
same federal indicator different scores.** That is a judgement call for you
and the authors, not something to resolve in the data.

Every other one of the twelve policy scores agrees: federal 3, 3, 3, 3, [3 vs
4], 3; state 2, 3, 4, 3, 4, 2.

## 3. New federal material with no Port Hedland equivalent at all

This is the largest gap. All of it is Commonwealth policy, so all of it applies
to Port Hedland exactly as it applies to Kwinana. Ordered roughly by how much
it matters to a Pilbara green iron assessment.

**Guarantee of Origin (3.1.6).** Launched November 2025, but the product
branch is open only to electrolytic hydrogen and no commencement date has been
set for the green metals standards. The draft calls this "the decisive
constraint on everything this section recommends: until green iron, green steel
and green aluminium can be certified, no Commonwealth green procurement mandate
can be written." Port Hedland's whole case is green iron — this is arguably
more important there than at Kwinana, and the site says nothing about it.

**Carbon Leakage Review and an Australian CBAM (3.1.1).** Released 13 February
2026, recommending a border adjustment mechanism covering cement and clinker
first, then steel, aluminium, lime, hydrogen and ammonia. The EU's own
mechanism entered its definitive regime on 1 January 2026. The Kwinana draft
also makes the sharper point in 3.1.4 that the EU mechanism deducts carbon
prices already paid in the country of production, so Safeguard liabilities
become a competitive asset rather than a cost. For iron and steel exported from
Port Hedland that reasoning transfers directly.

**The Net Zero Plan and its sector plans (3.1.1).** Australia's first
whole-of-economy decarbonisation framework. The Industry Sector Plan covers
nine subsectors including metals refining and iron and steel and is backed by a
$5 billion Net Zero Fund; the Resources Sector Plan projects sector emissions
falling to 19 Mt CO2‑e by 2050 but addresses Scope 1 only. The Resources plan is
the one that bears on the Pilbara, and the Scope 1 limitation is a real finding
for a region exporting ore.

**Environment Protection Reform Act 2025 and what follows (3.1.3).** Assent
1 December 2025 with six related bills — the largest overhaul of Commonwealth
environmental law since 1999. The National Environmental Protection Agency
commenced 1 July 2026; the first four National Environmental Standards were made
in August 2026; the substantive package (new approval pathways, a net-gain
offsets test, bioregional planning) does not commence until 1 December 2026.
Two consequences the draft draws out: nothing has been tested on a real project,
and a referral lodged after 1 December 2026 faces a different test from one
lodged before it. Also: from 1 December 2026 proponents must disclose scope 1
and 2 emissions above a threshold, but for transparency only — the disclosure
does not inform the approval decision.

**Bioregional plans and development zones (3.1.3).** Plans may designate zones
in which registered priority actions proceed without individual approval, with
coal and petroleum excluded but green industry and renewables not. Strategic
assessments have been expanded to landscape scale, and the 2026‑27 Budget put
$26.4 million behind bioregional plans and strategic assessments and $47.6
million behind state bilaterals. The Kwinana draft turns this into a concrete
ask — a bioregional plan or landscape-scale strategic assessment covering the
Western Trade Coast. **The equivalent Port Hedland ask writes itself: Boodarie
and the Eastern Pilbara industrial corridor.** That is the single most useful
thing to bring across, and the site's current recommendation (finalise the
reforms, add a climate trigger) is obsolete without it.

**Aboriginal cultural heritage as a federal schedule risk (3.1.3).** No
Commonwealth heritage reform has passed, so a section 10 declaration under the
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Heritage Protection Act 1984 remains an
unpredictable, project-stopping federal risk that no bilateral touches. The
draft judges it "arguably a larger schedule risk than the EPBC Act itself" for
WA renewables and industrial land. Given Port Hedland's native title and
heritage context — and the Yindjibarndi material already in its state sections
— this belongs there at least as strongly.

**The measured scale of the approvals problem (3.1.3).** Of 89 renewable energy
projects referred under the EPBC Act since 2023 that required assessment, only
one had a final ministerial decision at the last published count, with the
longest case running about five years. Port Hedland's approvals text asserts
delay without quantifying it.

**Capacity Investment Scheme mechanics (3.1.4).** Expanded to 40 GW in July
2025; first WA rounds in May 2026 awarded 1,886 MW of generation and 3,683 MWh
of storage across the SWIS. Port Hedland's argument is that the CIS does not
reach the NWIS — naming what it *did* award in the SWIS in the same round makes
that exclusion concrete rather than abstract.

**Critical Minerals Strategic Reserve (3.1.4).** Operational in the second half
of 2026 with $1.2 billion in offtake rights and contracts-for-difference, but
its initial mineral subset excludes lithium. Worth checking whether iron falls
inside or outside it — that determines whether it is a Port Hedland instrument
at all.

**Discretionary rescue as an investment signal (3.1.4).** Tomago's $2.5 billion
package in August 2026, Boyne Island's $1 billion in the 2026‑27 Budget,
Whyalla holding half the Green Iron Investment Fund. The draft's conclusion:
"For a precinct planning against published rules, discretionary rescue is a
worse signal than a modest but legislated credit." Port Hedland has no
incumbent smelter to be rescued, which arguably makes the point stronger there,
not weaker.

**Delivery not on track (3.1.1).** Government projections released 27 November
2025 put Australia at 42% below 2005 by 2030 and 48% by 2035, well short of the
NDC; the latest inventory has emissions 25.0% below 2005 with industrial
process emissions up 2.1%; Australia withdrew its COP31 bid in November 2025;
the 2026‑27 federal Budget cut clean energy programs across hydrogen, solar and
batteries while committing more than $10 billion to liquid fuel security. The
liquid fuel security commitment is a particularly awkward counterpoint to a
green iron thesis and should not be omitted from a Pilbara report.

**Safeguard Mechanism, current data (3.1.1).** The site is a compliance year
behind. It says "first full compliance year with covered emissions falling
approximately 2.7 Mt" and a review "scheduled for 2026‑27". Current: 2024‑25
data published 15 April 2026 shows 132.8 Mt CO2‑e across 208 facilities, down
2.3%, with offset surrenders up almost half — so compliance is increasingly met
through offsets rather than on-site abatement. The review **opened 7 August
2026**, submissions close 18 September 2026, and the live questions are the
post‑2030 baseline decline rate (4.9% a year against an estimated 6.6% needed)
and a possible cut in the coverage threshold from 100,000 to 25,000 tonnes.
The offsets finding and the threshold cut both bear heavily on the Pilbara,
where Port Hedland's own state section already argues that SERS leans on the
Safeguard in lieu of state compliance.

**AEMO's 2026 ISP (3.1.1).** Published 25 June 2026. Port Hedland cites the
*Draft* and states it "projects only around 75% renewable electricity by 2030".
The final finds the 82% target achievable on the optimal development path but
the current pipeline delivering only three-quarters of the capacity required;
under the Constrained Delivery sensitivity the target is missed, coal runs
longer and gas use rises. Those are different claims, and the site's version
conflates a renewable share with a share of required capacity.

**North West Shelf litigation (3.1.1).** The Federal Court challenge to the
extension was heard in July 2026 with judgment reserved. The site says only
that the approval "has not been reversed", which is true but incomplete now.

**Commonwealth Procurement Rules change (3.1.6).** 17 November 2025 —
non-corporate entities must invite only Australian businesses for non-panel
procurement below the relevant threshold, plus a supplier portal identifying
Indigenous, SME, Australian and women-owned businesses. The draft notes these
widen access rather than build capability, with no funded capability program
accompanying them — a distinction that strengthens Port Hedland's own
First Nations supply chain argument.

**CIS First Nations set-aside (3.1.6).** From July 2026 the scheme reserves
part of Tenders 9 and 10 for projects committing to at least five per cent
First Nations equity or revenue sharing, converting a scored criterion into a
qualifying threshold. Directly relevant to Port Hedland's Yindjibarndi
material.

**Where federal R&D money has actually landed (3.1.5).** The draft adds a
fourth structural weakness: very little of the named federal R&D money has
reached WA — ARENA's $19.8m to NeoSmelt and $2m to Firebird Metals in Perth,
with WA's largest federal clean energy win in the period, $814 million to
Murchison Green Hydrogen, being a production subsidy rather than research
funding. It concludes "a precinct readiness score should reflect what has
reached the ground." That test applies to Port Hedland too, and the Port
Hedland report's own list (Fortescue Solar Innovation Hub, PLS/Calix
Mid‑Stream, and the rest, per `PORT-HEDLAND-DRAFT-ISSUES.md`) is what would be
weighed against it.

**Smaller additions:** the Strategic Examination of R&D reported March 2026 as
*Ambitious Australia*, and the Budget lifted the R&D tax incentive threshold
and offset rates (3.1.5); CSIRO proceeding with the loss of up to 350 roles
(3.1.5); the 2026‑27 Budget redirecting $266.2 million of apprenticeship
employer incentives to SMEs and Group Training Organisations, Free TAFE made
permanent under the Free TAFE Act 2025 with its funding agreement expiring
30 June 2027 and WA's allocation falling from $17.9m to $13.4m, the $20 million
National Training Centre in New Energy Skills going to Victoria, and WA
receiving $5.1 million of the $35 million Clean Energy Capital Investment Fund
(3.1.2); the Net Zero Economy Authority developing a transition monitoring
framework with UTS and CSIRO, unpublished (3.1.2); the National Renewable
Energy Priority List, which added three SWIS wind farms in July 2026 — a
SWIS-only instrument so far, which is itself a finding for the Pilbara (3.1.3);
FMiA's five priority sectors named, and March 2026 regulations extending
critical minerals eligibility to high-purity alumina and cathode-active
material (3.1.4).

**One structural explanation worth adapting rather than copying (3.1.2).** The
draft explains *why* no federal transition program targets displaced industrial
workers: the Net Zero Economy Authority's Energy Industry Jobs Plan covers
closing coal and gas-fired power stations and their dependent employers, which
is why Collie gets coordinated support and an alumina refinery cannot qualify.
Port Hedland's equivalent question is different — diesel-to-electric mining
transition rather than a closure — but the same trigger analysis is what makes
the finding evidenced rather than asserted.

## 4. State policy: where the two documents disagree

Port Hedland's six state indicators are genuinely region-specific and, on the
whole, better written than the Kwinana draft's. But several factual conflicts
need resolving, because both describe the same WA government.

### 3.2.1 Emissions reduction — two real conflicts

**Is WA's emissions trend flat or rising?** Port Hedland: "emissions have
plateaued in recent years around 89.4 Mt CO2‑e (roughly 17% above 2005 levels),
this stability hides a significant gap." New Kwinana draft: "State emissions
reached 90.94 Mt CO2‑e in 2024, a 2% rise on the previous year and the third
consecutive annual increase." These cannot both stand. If the new figure is
right, Port Hedland's "plateaued" framing understates the problem in the region
that produces 40% of the state's emissions.

**Which bill?** Port Hedland: "The Climate Action this Decade Bill 2025
includes a 2030 target, but its passage through Parliament remains
unconfirmed", and recommends WA "must pass the Climate Action this Decade Bill
2025 with whole-of-economy interim targets." New Kwinana draft: the Climate
Change Bill 2023 lapsed, and in May 2026 the Government confirmed it is
replacing it with a **Clean Energy Powerhouse Bill that abandons interim
emissions targets altogether**, legislating green commodity export, CCS and
renewable generation targets instead — with the Premier acknowledging the
approach may see WA's emissions rise relative to the rest of the country. The
Bill had not been introduced at the time of writing; reporting against the new
targets is flagged for mid‑2027.

Port Hedland's recommendation therefore asks Parliament to pass a bill that,
on the new draft's account, has been superseded by one designed to do the
opposite. This is the most consequential single correction in the state
sections. Note also that `PORT-HEDLAND-DRAFT-ISSUES.md` already records a
related mismatch in the Port Hedland report — reference entry [7] titled
*Climate Change Bill 2023* against a sentence about the *Climate Action this
Decade Bill 2025* — which now looks like the same confusion surfacing twice.

**Missing from Port Hedland, and it is Pilbara money:** the Kwinana draft cites
"a $3 million allocation to support Pilbara Traditional Owners" as evidence of
proactive First Nations engagement. A Pilbara-specific commitment sitting in
the Kwinana report and absent from the Port Hedland one. Also absent: the
$1.4 billion Clean Energy Fund in the 2026‑27 State Budget, and the fact that
the Strategic Industries Fund was *doubled* to $1 billion in June 2025 (Port
Hedland cites the $1 billion without the doubling).

Port Hedland is stronger than Kwinana here on the PoweringWA Community
Benefits Guideline being SWIS-only, and on the Yindjibarndi 3 GW / 25–50%
equity agreement. Keep both.

### 3.2.2 Skills and training

Missing from Port Hedland, all statewide and so applicable:

- The **Clean Energy Skills National Centre of Excellence**, a $70.5 million
  initiative now operating across the TAFE network. Not mentioned anywhere in
  the Port Hedland evidence.
- The **WA Skills and Workforce Development Action Plan** — the draft's point
  that it is "too broad to address the specific energy transition needs" of a
  single region transfers directly to Port Hedland's argument that no
  region-specific plan exists.
- **$124 million in the 2026‑27 State Budget** for lower-fee and Fee-Free TAFE
  into 2027, within a $216 million TAFE and training package. Port Hedland
  currently says "$100 million for lower-fee and Fee-Free TAFE" — likely
  superseded, worth checking which year each figure belongs to.
- The **near-term delivery constraint** paragraph: no state program addresses
  the resources-sector wage differential driving attrition from clean energy
  trades, and apprenticeship throughput operates on a horizon mismatched to
  the construction window. In the Pilbara, where the wage differential
  originates, this argument is stronger than at Kwinana. Port Hedland raises
  diesel-fleet electrification but not the wage differential.

Not worth porting: the $24.1 million South Metropolitan TAFE Munster upgrade
(Kwinana catchment).

Internal tidy: Port Hedland's 3.2.2 presents CETRI as "being established"
while its own 3.2.5 says CETRI "remains pre-operational, with first intake not
expected until 2027". The 2027 date should appear in both.

### 3.2.3 Approvals

**Conflict on the Renewable Energy Planning Code.** Port Hedland: "remains
pending, and is not expected to be incorporated into planning schemes until
after August 2026" — stated twice, including in the recommendation. New Kwinana
draft: "remained in draft at the time of writing, with consultation having
closed on 10 April 2026 and **no finalisation or gazettal date announced**."
Port Hedland asserts a timeline the newer draft says does not exist.

**Missing dates that Port Hedland should carry.** The State Development Act
2025 commenced 19 February 2026; the Western Trade Coast was announced as WA's
first State Development Area on 16 April 2026 and gazetted 26 June 2026, with
the first five Priority Projects being NeoSmelt and four Wheatbelt and Mid West
wind farms. Port Hedland's 3.2.4 makes the right point — none of the first
cohort is a Pilbara site — but its 3.2.3 discusses the Act without the
commencement or gazettal dates.

**Named examples.** Port Hedland says "several large projects progressed from
lodgement to approval in three to five months". The Kwinana draft names them:
Tathra ~3–4 months in late 2025 without formal environmental assessment, Trina
Solar's Killawarra ~3 months, Narrogin BESS ~5 months. Naming them makes the
claim checkable.

**A tension worth exploiting rather than smoothing.** The Kwinana draft's
3.2.3 says Tathra cleared *state* approval in three to four months; its 3.1.3
says Synergy referred the same 1,000 MW Tathra Wind Farm under the EPBC Act in
April 2026 and it remained under Commonwealth assessment four months later.
Those are two different tracks, not a contradiction — but read together they
are the sharpest available illustration of Port Hedland's own argument that
state reform does not relieve federal exposure. Port Hedland already makes that
argument with Fortescue's Turner River Solar Hub; Tathra doubles it.

**Missing ask.** The Kwinana draft recommends "publishing aggregate approval
timeline data by project type". Port Hedland does not ask for this, and it is a
cheap, verifiable reform.

Port Hedland is better than Kwinana here on the Mining Act 1978 displacing
development approval, and on Pilbara tenure and native title extending the
pre-lodgement window. Keep both. Port Hedland also omits that PoweringWA "now
functions as a dedicated delivery body for grid-connected projects" — statewide,
worth a clause.

### 3.2.4 Investment framework

**Internal contradiction inside Port Hedland, on Rewiring the Nation.** Its
3.2.4 says "WA's **full allocation** of up to $3 billion in concessional
Rewiring the Nation financing for transmission is earmarked **specifically for
Pilbara infrastructure** rather than split state-wide". Its 3.2.1 says the
Pilbara Energy Transition Plan corridors "aim to … unlock **part of** WA's
allocation of up to $3 billion". The new Kwinana draft supports neither
strongly — it says only that up to $3 billion is available for transmission
upgrades, with the Pilbara Energy Transition Plan identifying priority
corridors. The "full allocation, Pilbara-earmarked" claim is the strongest of
the three and needs a source or softening.

**Internal contradiction inside Port Hedland, on the Boodarie budget line.**
3.2.4 says "$27.1 million in the 2026‑27 State Budget for water supply studies
**and** infrastructure corridor design at Boodarie" (and 3.2.1 repeats it).
3.2.5 separates them: "$27.1 million for planning and design of the Boodarie
SIA … alongside **$23.8 million** to investigate water supply options", within
a $91.7–92 million Strategic Industries Fund tranche. The Kwinana draft gives
the tranche as $91.7 million across Western Trade Coast, Kemerton and Boodarie,
of which $53.3 million is for Latitude 32 and the AMC. Three sections, two
different splits — needs one reading of the Budget papers.

**Name the instrument.** The Kwinana draft identifies the **Renewable Energy
Transformation Agreement** as the centrepiece of WA's investment architecture:
the Commonwealth underwriting 6.5 TWh of cumulative renewable generation and a
minimum 1.1 GW (4.4 GWh equivalent) of dispatchable storage. Port Hedland
argues the Pilbara is excluded from the CIS but never names the agreement it is
excluded from. Naming it sharpens the finding considerably.

**Missing statewide instruments available to Pilbara proponents:** the New
Energies Industries Funding Stream (up to $60 million per round) and the **Made
in WA Energy Affordability Investment Program** — $153.3 million in low-interest
loans for clean manufacturing and energy efficiency, announced March 2026,
design consultation closed 3 July 2026, applications not yet open. Neither
appears in the Port Hedland evidence. Also absent: WA Treasury Corporation
issuing sustainability bonds aligned with green finance principles.

**Worth noting rather than porting:** the 2026‑27 Budget's $85 million for
green iron and steel including $75 million for the NeoSmelt pilot. The money
goes to Kwinana, but the *existence* of a state green iron line item is
material to Port Hedland, which has the POSCO/Marubeni/China Steel HBI plant
and Boodarie and receives none of it. That asymmetry is a finding.

**Align the recommendation.** The Kwinana draft's first ask is legislating an
interim emissions target, explicitly noting "a gap the Clean Energy Powerhouse
Bill would leave open". Port Hedland's 3.2.4 recommendation does not mention
the legislated-target gap at all, even though its own 3.2.1 makes the point.

**Copy faults in Port Hedland's own text, found while comparing.** The AREH
paragraph opens with a comma splice: "The region's flagship renewable hydrogen
project has also weakened, AREH — a roughly $55 billion …". And the sentence
"Investors backing renewable generation or storage in the Pilbara **therefore**
have no equivalent underwriting mechanism" sits at the end of the Rewiring the
Nation paragraph, where nothing about underwriting precedes it — the premise is
two paragraphs earlier. It reads as a paragraph seam that moved.

### 3.2.5 Research and development

Port Hedland's version is fully rewritten, Pilbara-specific and considerably
better than the Kwinana draft's, which is still in note form. Three items to
take from Kwinana anyway:

- The Clean Energy Future Fund has delivered **approximately $26.4 million
  across three completed rounds**, and the 2026‑27 Budget **topped the fund up
  by a further $18 million to 2030‑31**. Port Hedland has the fourth round's
  $9 million but neither the cumulative figure nor the top-up.
- Carbon Innovation Grants Round 2 awarded **$6.58 million across ten
  projects** in December 2025. Port Hedland names the two Pilbara-anchored
  projects but not the round total, which is what shows the Pilbara's share.
- The fourth CEFF round closed **20 April 2026** — Port Hedland says only
  "April 2026".

Port Hedland is right to keep the Trilateral Hydrogen Hub Feasibility Study as
the only substantive international collaboration, and right that it has no
Pilbara equivalent. The Mid West Hydrogen Hub ($55 million, Oakajee SIA, six
proponents) is a reasonable comparator to add if the section has room.

### 3.2.6 Procurement

Port Hedland's version is much stronger — Buy Local Zone 3 price preferences,
the Aboriginal Procurement Policy's 10% Pilbara target against 2% metropolitan,
the Pilbara Development Commission Local Content Adviser, RED Grants Round 8,
and the Auditor General finding. None of that is in the Kwinana draft, and the
Kwinana draft's version still carries promotional language ("excellent",
"seeds the Future State") that Port Hedland's has properly stripped.

**But Port Hedland omits the most consequential thing in the Kwinana draft.**
Kwinana: "In November 2025 the Government went beyond guidance, **mandating an
'if not, why not' preference for locally manufactured green steel on major
government projects**, committing to a WAIPS addendum for steel, and directing
Western Power, Synergy and Horizon Power to prioritise local supply." Port
Hedland records only "the forthcoming WAIPS Addendum for steel and local-content
directions to Western Power, Synergy, and Horizon Power … remains at the
announced stage". The "if not, why not" green steel preference is the actual
policy change, it is arguably the most Port Hedland-relevant procurement
development in the round, and the Port Hedland page does not mention it. It
also matters for the score reasoning: Port Hedland holds at 2 because
"procurement criteria still do not require low-emission suppliers", and an
"if not, why not" preference is the closest WA has come to exactly that.

## 5. Going the other way — Port Hedland material the Kwinana draft should take

Flagging these since they are the same two jurisdictions:

- **The Fuel Tax Credit scheme.** `PORT-HEDLAND-DRAFT-ISSUES.md` records a
  substantial new paragraph in the Port Hedland report — $10.2 billion in
  2024‑25, mining's share around $4.8 billion, BHP's $622 million claim against
  $17 million of Safeguard compliance credits, Fortescue's proposed $50 million
  cap. The new Kwinana draft has nothing on fuel tax credits. It is a federal
  instrument and a direct fossil fuel subsidy sitting inside a federal
  emissions commitment assessment; its absence from the Kwinana draft looks
  like an omission rather than a judgement.
- **The approval bilateral correction.** Both documents now say WA has an
  assessment bilateral only, so this is settled — but the Kwinana draft should
  carry the 21 April 2026 MoU signatories and the "assessment bilateral first,
  approvals later" sequencing that Port Hedland's text has.
- **Why the CIS does not reach the NWIS.** Port Hedland's evidence now gives
  both halves (no wholesale spot market and no capacity mechanism, per the
  ACCC's December 2024 Pilbara ISOCo determination). The Kwinana draft does not
  need this, but the Kwinana *report* asserts CIS coverage of the SWIS in
  3.1.4 and 3.2.4 without noting that the scheme is WEM-bounded — which is what
  makes the Pilbara exclusion structural rather than incidental.

## 7. Faults in the site's Port Hedland evidence, found along the way

Independent of the comparison, worth fixing whenever these five indicators are
next touched:

  `state-rd`. A Word artefact from the `---` in the source document.
- **Citation markers out of order in `federal-emissions-commitment`.** The
  sequence runs … 15, 16, **20**, 17, 18,19. Twenty entries exist so nothing
  renders as a dead link, but the house convention numbers markers in the order
  a reader meets them, and `[20]` currently appears fourth from last. The

  `report: null`, so it looks like the new entry was appended to the list
